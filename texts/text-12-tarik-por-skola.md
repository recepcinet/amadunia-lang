# Tarik por skola

*The road to school.*

The twelfth text, and the first written **for the imperative**. The rule was
granted on September 3, 2026 — [a sentence that begins with a verb and has no
subject is a command](../grammar/sentence-types.md) — and until this page
nothing had been written to use it. [Text 8](text-8-kaifa-suru-ca.md) was
written deliberately without one, to measure what the absence cost; this is the
other half of that experiment.

A mother walks a child to school. It is the situation a command belongs to:
somebody who knows the road telling somebody who does not.

Written at 300 roots, with no grammar beyond Lesson 25.

---

```
Pagi. Mama aur anak go skola.

Ambil mano mi.
Anda una mi.
No kimbia.

Stop! Lihat tarik.
Es auto. Auto go hayai.
Espera sini.

Auto suda go. Tarik kosong sasa.
Go sasa.

Anak: Mi kan skola!
Mama: Ya. Skola in tarik ini.

Anak: Mi mau kimbia.
Mama: Kimbia. No kimbia hayai.

Anak kimbia skola.
Mama lihat anak.

Mama: Studi hao! Dengar mualim!
Anak: Ya, mama! Bai!
Mama: Bai. Mi espera yu in dom.
```

## What the imperative did

**Eleven of the twenty-seven sentences are commands**, and not one of them
needed a word that did not already exist. *Ambil mano mi*, *Anda una mi*, *No
kimbia*, *Stop*, *Lihat tarik*, *Espera sini*, *Go sasa*, *Kimbia*, *No kimbia
hayai*, *Studi hao*, *Dengar mualim*.

Put beside [text 8](text-8-kaifa-suru-ca.md), where twelve of sixteen sentences
open with *yu* because nothing else could carry an instruction, the difference
is the whole argument for the rule. Here *yu* appears **once**, in *Mi espera yu
in dom*, where it is a real object and not a stand-in for a missing form.

**The other half of the same rule turned up without being asked for.** Five of
the twenty-seven are fragments — *Pagi.*, *Ya.*, *Ya, mama!*, *Bai!*, *Bai.* —
which is the [constituent-alone rule](../grammar/sentence-types.md) settled on
the same day. Neither was planned into this page; a road and a goodbye produced
them.

**Negation needed nothing new either.** *No kimbia* and *No kimbia hayai* put
*no* in front of the verb exactly as a statement does, and *No kimbia hayai*
also carries an adverb — the order is *no* → verb → adverb, which is the same
order the full sentence rule gives.

**A command and a statement never collide here.** *Mi mau kimbia* is a
statement because it has a subject; *Kimbia* is a command because it has none.
The two stand in consecutive lines of the dialogue and no reader can mix them.

## Gaps

**There is still no way to tell someone to *be* something,** and this page
wanted it three times:

- *Be careful* at the road — written as *Stop! Lihat tarik*, two commands for
  one.
- *Be quick* when the road cleared — written as *Go sasa*, which says when and
  not how.
- *Do not be afraid* of the car — impossible twice over: *korku* is a noun, and
  a sentence-initial *es* is the existential.

That is [the open question the command rule left](../grammar/sentence-types.md),
and this is the first page to hit it by writing rather than by reasoning. Three
wants on one page is the strongest evidence it has.

**No preposition for *near*.** *The school is near the tree* could not be
written, so the text says *Skola in tarik ini* — the school is on this road —
which is true but is not what a person pointing at a tree would say. *dekat* is
an adjective, and using it in front of a noun would be inventing a preposition.
[Already on the open list](../grammar/place.md), found again here.

**No word for *then*.** The car passes, the road empties, the child runs; the
order on the page is doing the work. Recorded with
[the other gaps the writing has asked for](../dictionary/README.md#words-the-writing-has-asked-for),
where [text 8](text-8-kaifa-suru-ca.md) put it first.
