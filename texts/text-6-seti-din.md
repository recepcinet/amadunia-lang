# Seti din

*Seven days.*

The sixth text. A child is ill, and is taken to the hospital.

It was written to reach the part of the dictionary nothing had reached. Five
roots — *kulit*, *yanlis*, *foto*, *ba*, *nau* — appeared nowhere in a
sentence in the whole repository: they existed as a dictionary row and
nothing else. Forty-five more appeared exactly once. Most of them were body
parts, illness words, and the high numbers. A doctor's visit is where those
three meet without being forced together.

Written at 300 roots, with no grammar beyond Lesson 23.

---

```
Anak bimar. Ta bimar seti din.
Sar ta garam. Kulit ta garam tena.
Ta no mau kula. Ta no mau lala.
Mama ta espera. Anak no lebi hao.

Mama aur anak go hospital pagi ini.
Es cok insan in hospital.
Nau insan espera in kamra baru.
Ta-ta espera du hora.

Doktor buka mulut anak. Ta lihat dente.
Ta lihat ain aur telinga. Ta senti kulit anak.
Kulit garam.
Mesin ambil foto korpo anak. Doktor lihat foto.

Mama tanya: "Kalb anak mal?"
Doktor respon: "No. Kalb anak hao.
Korpo ta kuat. Ide yu yanlis."
Doktor beri ilac por mama.
"Anak nomu ilac ba din."

Anak nomu ilac ba din. Ta lala cok. Ta kula tena.
Kulit ta no garam. Ta no bimar.
Anak go skola tena. Ta senang.
```

## What the text found

**A machine takes a photo of a body.** *Mesin ambil foto korpo anak.* The
language has no word for an X-ray and did not need one: a machine, a picture,
a body. Nothing was lost here — though calling it compounding, as this note
first did, was wrong: it is a sentence, and [the language forms no compound
words](../grammar/word-formation.md).

**No copula anywhere in twelve predicates.** *Sar ta garam*, *Korpo ta kuat*,
*Kulit garam* — every one of these is a noun and an adjective with nothing
between them. English needs *is* twelve times here; Amadunia needs it zero
times, because *es* is only for noun predicates. It appears once in the whole
text, and there it means *there are*.

**The high numbers work like the low ones.** *seti din*, *ba din*, *nau
insan* — seven, eight and nine behave exactly as *du* does, which is the
whole claim of the number system and had never actually been shown.

## Gaps

Three things this text wanted to say and could not. None of them was invented
to make the text work.

**There is no word for pain.** (First on [the list](../dictionary/README.md#words-the-writing-has-asked-for).) The child is ill for seven days and never says
where it hurts, because there is no way to. The nearest root is *mal*, bad,
which is not the same thing — a bad leg is not a leg that hurts. This is a
dictionary gap, not a grammar gap, and it is the first one found by writing
rather than by counting. Recorded for the vocabulary after A1.

**Ordinals are still open, and this text is the clearest case yet.** The
story runs on days: ill seven days, medicine eight days, back to school after
that. It wants *on the eighth day* and *on the ninth day*, and can only say
*for eight days*. The line "Anak go skola tena" — the child goes to school
again — is a retreat from a date to an adverb. See the open question in
[grammar/README.md](../grammar/README.md).

**~~A doctor cannot tell a patient to open their mouth.~~ It can now.** The
examination is written from outside — *Doktor buka mulut anak*, the doctor
opens the child's mouth — because on the day it was written there was no
settled imperative to write it from inside. This paragraph was one of the sites
that argued for one, and unlike the sites counted in the briefing it was found
by writing rather than by grep. The rule arrived on September 3, 2026:
[a verb with no subject is a command](../grammar/sentence-types.md), so *Buka
mulut* is now sayable. The text is left as written, because what it records is
what the absence cost.
