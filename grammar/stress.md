# Stress

*Taught on [the front page](../README.md) and in [Lesson 01](../lessons/lesson-01-greetings.md).*

*Status: settled — decided September 3, 2026.*

## The rule

**Stress falls on the second-to-last syllable of every word.**

A one-syllable word carries it on the syllable it has. There is no exception,
no marked word, and nothing to memorise per word.

| | |
|---|---|
| **DU**-nia | world |
| a-**MA**-dunia → a-ma-**DU**-nia | the language's own name |
| **MER**-si | thank you |
| ka-**LI**-ma | word |
| sa-**FA**-ri | journey |
| **MI** | I |
| **ES** | is |

## What counts as a syllable

**One syllable per vowel group. A vowel pair is one nucleus, not two.**

This is the question that had to be settled first, and it is settled by the
phonology rather than by choice: only five vowel pairs exist — *ai, ao, au, ia,
ua* — three vowels in a row are forbidden, and those five are exactly the
sequences the source languages pronounce as a single glide. Counting them as
one nucleus makes syllable-counting mechanical: **count the vowel groups.**

| | Syllables | |
|---|---|---|
| *dunia* | 2 | du-nia, not du-ni-a |
| *akua* | 2 | a-kua |
| *mausim* | 2 | mau-sim |
| *familia* | 3 | fa-mi-lia |
| *uan* | 1 | one vowel group |

Under this definition the 300 roots are 57 of one syllable, 209 of two and 34
of three.

## What stress does to the sound

**It makes the syllable louder and a little higher. It does not change it.**

No vowel reduces, none lengthens, and none shifts quality. The *a* in *ama* is
the same *a* whether it is stressed or not, and *dunia* said with the beat on
*DU* has exactly the sounds spelled in it. This is how Indonesian, Swahili and
Japanese work, and it is the opposite of English, Russian and Portuguese, where
unstressed vowels collapse toward a schwa.

That is not a stylistic preference. Design rule 2 says the language is written
as it is spoken; a reduced vowel would break it the first time anyone said a
word out loud, because the letter on the page would no longer be the sound in
the air. Stress had to be the kind that leaves the spelling true, and this kind
is. See [pronunciation.md](pronunciation.md), where the vowels are.

## The hyphen does not join words for stress

A hyphen joins two words for meaning — a plural, or a number. Each half keeps
its own stress.

| Written | Said | |
|---|---|---|
| anak-anak | **A**-nak **A**-nak | children |
| mi-mi | both halves stressed, each on its only syllable | we, not including you |
| des-uan | **DES** **U**-an | eleven |

## Why the second-to-last

**It is what the largest source families already do.** Indonesian and Malay,
Swahili, Polish and Quechua stress the penultimate as a rule, and it is the
default in Spanish and Italian. Austronesian is the family with the widest
reach in this dictionary, and Niger-Congo is not far behind.

**It is what the one constructed language that has actually been spoken for a
century does.** Esperanto has stressed the penultimate since 1887.

**It is the most forgiving choice available.** In **266 of the 300 roots — 89%
— the second-to-last syllable is also the first**, because 57 roots have one
syllable and 209 have two. A learner whose own language stresses the beginning
of a word is right about nine words in ten without being taught anything, and
can only go wrong on the 34 three-syllable roots.

## Rejected

**Initial — always the first syllable.** Czech, Hungarian, Finnish, Icelandic,
Latvian. It agrees with the penultimate on 89% of the dictionary anyway, so it
buys almost nothing, and it disagrees with Austronesian and Swahili — the two
families this language leans on hardest — on the third where it differs.

**Final — always the last.** Turkish, Persian, French, Armenian, Hebrew. A real
tradition and a large one, but it is the option that disagrees with the
penultimate everywhere except one-syllable words, and it disagrees with
Esperanto, Indonesian, Malay and Swahili at once. Choosing it would mean the
widest-reach family in the dictionary is also the one whose speakers say every
word wrong.

**Free — no rule.** What the language did until today, and
[the poem](../texts/text-5-uan.md) is why it could not stay that way: *DIN lai*
and *din LAI* are different lines, so nothing could be sung, scanned or taught
by ear.

## What it unblocked, the same day

[The poem](../texts/text-5-uan.md) was written while this was undecided and
said so — being said aloud was "the thing the language is least ready for". It
was scanned within the hour, and two things came out of it that nobody could
have seen before. Its paired lines match to the syllable, 6 and 6, 4 and 4, 6
and 6, 5 and 5, though the poem was built on parallel words and nothing counted
beats. And its closing line, *Kita ama dunia una*, is the only regular line in
it: **X . X . X . X .**, four trochees with nothing left over. That was luck
rather than craft, because when the line was written no rule could say where
its beats were.

## Nothing was at risk — and what it cost anyway

**No two words in Amadunia are told apart by stress, and none ever can be** —
that would be a minimal pair, which the language forbids. No word changed
meaning, and no text needed rewriting.

But a rule that fits one tradition cannot fit them all, and it is worth saying
plainly who pays. Taking the first source each entry names, and counting only
roots of two syllables or more:

| | Roots |
|---|---|
| First source stresses the penultimate — Indonesian, Malay, Swahili, Spanish, Italian, Polish | **87**, and they keep their beat |
| First source stresses the last — Turkish, Persian, French | **34**, and their beat moves |

*anahtar* is a-nah-**TAR** in Turkish and a-**NAH**-tar here. *pencere* is
pen-ce-**RE** and becomes pen-**CE**-re. *ates*, *yatak*, *korku*, *burun*,
*bage*, *mersi* all shift the same way. A Turkish or Persian speaker meets
eleven per cent of the dictionary saying their own word with the beat in the
wrong place.

That is the real price of the decision, it is not zero, and nothing makes it
smaller: any single rule moves somebody's beat. It is paid to the smaller side —
87 against 34, nearly three to one — and what is lost is recognition of rhythm,
never meaning, because meaning never rode on stress here in the first place.

(French is counted as final-stress by simplification: it stresses the phrase
rather than the word, so *mersi* has no fixed word beat to move.)

## Open questions

- **Whether the short grammar words reduce in speech.** The rule stresses every
  word, so *Mi ama dunia* is **MI A**-ma **DU**-nia, with three stresses in
  three words. Most languages let particles like *es, no, aur, in, dari, por*
  lean on the word beside them instead. Whether Amadunia does is a fact about
  how it is spoken, not about how it is written, and nothing here decides it.
