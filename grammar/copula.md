# The copula — saying "is"

*The word is given in [Lesson 06](../lessons/lesson-06-possession.md); the rule is taught in [Lesson 11](../lessons/lesson-11-being.md), and [Lesson 12](../lessons/lesson-12-colors-directions.md) drills it with a deliberately wrong sentence.*

*The existential also wins the sentence-initial slot over [the command rule](sentence-types.md): a sentence beginning with* es *is "there is", never "be".*

*Status: settled — decided September 2, 2026. Reversible if the founder disagrees; see "Why this and not the alternatives" below.*

## The rule

**Before a noun, *es*. Before anything else, nothing.**

| Predicate is a… | Amadunia | English |
|---|---|---|
| noun | Mi **es** doktor. | I am a doctor. |
| noun | Sol **es** rafiki mi. | Sol is my friend. |
| adjective | Dom kabir. | The house is big. |
| adjective | Ca garam. | The tea is hot. |
| place word | Mi sini. | I am here. |
| place word | Hotel situ. | The hotel is there. |

*es* is a verb. It appears when the thing after the subject is a noun, and only then.

## Why a noun needs it and an adjective does not

Because of the possession rule. Two nouns side by side already mean something: the second owns the first.

| | |
|---|---|
| Rafiki mi doktor. | my friend's doctor — *a thing, not a sentence* |
| Rafiki mi **es** doktor. | My friend is a doctor. — *a sentence* |
| Nama mi Sol. | Sol's my-name — *nonsense* |
| Nama mi **es** Sol. | My name is Sol. |

Without *es*, *Sol doktor* can only be "Sol's doctor". With it, "Sol is a doctor". The two readings never touch.

An adjective cannot own anything, so *dom kabir* has no possessive reading to collide with. It needs no *es*, and it takes none.

## Tense

*es* is a verb, so it takes the tense particles like any verb:

| | |
|---|---|
| Mi **suda es** doktor. | I was a doctor. |
| Ta **saufa es** rafiki yu. | She will be your friend. |

Adjectives and place words take the tense particle **directly** — in a sentence, they do the work of a verb:

| | |
|---|---|
| Dom **suda** kabir. | The house was big. |
| Ca **saufa** garam. | The tea will be hot. |
| Mi **suda** sini. | I was here. |

There is no *suda es kabir*. An adjective is its own verb.

## Adjective: sentence or phrase?

*dom kabir* standing alone is a sentence: "the house is big". Inside a longer sentence it is a phrase: *Mi kan dom kabir* — "I see a big house". The difference is whether anything comes before or after it. This is exactly how Indonesian *rumah besar* works, and it has served three hundred million people without a copula.

## In questions

The question word takes the slot of what it asks about, so *es* stays where it is:

| | |
|---|---|
| Nama yu **es ke**? | What is your name? |
| Yu **es kim**? | Who are you? |
| Ta-ta **es kim**? | Who are they? |
| Dom kabir? | Is the house big? — *adjective, no es* |
| Hotel nali? | Where is the hotel? — *place word, no es* |

## Sources

*es* is the oldest and most widely shared verb on Earth. Spanish *es*, Latin *est*, Italian *è*, French *est*, Portuguese *é*, German *ist*, English *is*, Dutch *is*, Russian *est'*, Polish *jest*, Hindi *hai*, Persian *ast*, Greek *eínai*, Sanskrit *asti* — every one from the same root, spoken today by some three billion people.

The split — a copula with nouns, none with adjectives — is the Chinese rule (*wǒ shì yīshēng* but *fángzi dà*), the Swahili rule (*mimi ni daktari* but adjective agreement), the Haitian rule (*mwen se doktè* but *kay la gwo*). Zero copula with adjectives alone is the majority pattern: Russian, Arabic, Hebrew, Turkish, Hungarian, Indonesian, Japanese, Korean, Thai, Vietnamese.

## Why this and not the alternatives

**Why not zero everywhere.** Arabic, Russian and Hebrew manage it, but they have case, agreement or free word order to tell "X is Y" from "X's Y". Amadunia has none of those; it has one word order and a possession rule that makes *noun noun* possessive. Zero copula with nouns was already producing ambiguous sentences in Lessons 6, 7, 9 and 10 — all corrected in this decision.

**Why not *es* everywhere.** It would add a word to every adjective sentence the language has spoken since Lesson 3, for no gain: adjectives were not ambiguous when this was decided. And the tense rule for adjectives (*dom suda kabir*) falls out for free without it. That parenthesis said *never* until September 5, 2026; the fragment rule of September 3 made it untrue, and the open question below is the correction.

**Why *es* and not another word:**

| Candidate | Source | Reason rejected |
|---|---|---|
| ni | Swahili *ni* | minimal pair with *mi* |
| da | Japanese *da* | d/t pair with *ta* |
| dir | Turkish *-dir* | minimal pair with *din* (day) |
| si | Chinese *shì* 是 | prefix of *sis* (6) and *sini*; and "yes" in Spanish and Italian, colliding in sense with *ya* |
| se | Haitian *se* | prefix of *sen*, *sema*, *seti*; recognised by few |
| adala | Indonesian *adalah* | three syllables for the commonest verb in the language |
| i | Tok Pisin *i* | one bare vowel; too light to hear |

*es* has no collision and the widest recognition of any candidate by a factor of ten.

## Open questions

- **A noun with an adjective is now both a sentence and a phrase.** *Dom
  merah* is "the house is red" by this rule, and since
  [the fragment rule](sentence-types.md) of September 3, 2026 granted that any
  constituent may stand alone as an utterance, it is also "the red house"
  standing on its own. Nothing distinguishes them. **139 two-word utterances
  in the repository have this shape**, counted everywhere but the two pages
  that state the figure — writing the example *Dom merah* into this very
  bullet made the first draft of the count wrong by one, which is the third
  time this repository's own prose has become its evidence. [`check.py`](../check.py) recounts it rather than trusting this page.

  The corpus reads only one of them the other way, and it was found by reading
  Lesson 12 end to end: that lesson's own table glosses *Dom merah.* as "The
  house is red", and eighty lines later its conversation glossed the identical
  string "The red house." The gloss was corrected; the ambiguity was not,
  because correcting it needs a decision. The two greetings *Pagi hao!* and
  *Aksam hao!* are the other side of it and are not a problem — an idiomatic
  "Good morning!" for what the words say literally is ordinary translation.

  So this is a formal ambiguity that context has so far carried, exactly like
  the third option in [proposal-names.md](proposal-names.md). It is recorded,
  not briefed: it may well need nothing.

  **Two scanners for it were written and both rejected.** Requiring one string
  to keep one gloss inside a lesson finds two hits, and both are the deliberate
  statement-and-question pairs (*Yu mau ca.* / *Yu mau ca?*) that teach rising
  tone — keep the punctuation and it finds nothing, including the Lesson 12
  fault, which lives in a conversation's paragraph translation rather than a
  table row. Aligning a paragraph translation to the lines above it is guesswork.
  Neither was added; a check that cannot catch the fault that prompted it is
  worse than no check, because it reads as cover.

- **"want to be" plus an adjective has no form.** *es* is a verb and can be chained — *mau es doktor*, "want to be a doctor". An adjective is its own predicate and takes no verb, so *mau* has nothing to attach to: "wants to be the easiest" cannot be built. Both [text 3](../texts/text-3-lingua-ini.md) and Lesson 21 hit this and both were rewritten around it — text 3 by supplying a noun (*mau es lingua paling asan*), Lesson 21 by dropping the modal. Whether *es* is allowed before an adjective inside a verb chain, or something else is needed, is undecided. Found September 3, 2026 by checking every sentence in the repository against the copula rule.

- ~~Verb chaining~~ — settled in [verb-chains.md](verb-chains.md): the verbs simply follow one another, *Mi mau kula pan*.

- ~~Negation~~ — settled in [negation.md](negation.md): *Mi no es doktor*. *no* goes before *es*, and before the tense particle when there is one.
- ~~Existence~~ — settled September 3, 2026: **es at the front of the sentence, with no subject, means "there is".**

  | | |
  |---|---|
  | Es hotel in madina. | There is a hotel in the city. |
  | No es ca. | There is no tea. |
  | Es dom kabir sini? | Is there a big house here? |
  | Suda es skola in negara ini. | There was a school in this country. |

  No new word. Amadunia never drops a subject, so a sentence beginning with *es* can only be existential. Spanish *hay*, French *il y a*, Portuguese *tem*, Turkish *var* and Chinese *yǒu* all press an existing verb into this job; *punya* (to have) was the alternative and was rejected because it needs an owner, and existence has none.
