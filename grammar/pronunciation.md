# Pronunciation

*Taught on [the front page](../README.md) and in [Lesson 01](../lessons/lesson-01-greetings.md).*

*Status: written down September 3, 2026. Most of it was already decided and had never been said.*

## What this file fixes

Amadunia's second design rule is **one letter, one sound**. Until now the
language had named that sound for exactly two of its twenty letters — `c` and
`y` — and left the other eighteen, including all five vowels, to be guessed.

Nothing here is a new decision. Three hundred roots with sourced etymologies
already commit the language to these values: *ca* comes from Chinese *chá*,
*dunia* from Arabic and Swahili, *kita* from Indonesian, *dom* from Russian.
Every one of those languages reads these letters the same way, and the
README's own test — "if you can say *banana*" — pins the vowels. This file
reads the values off the vocabulary rather than choosing them.

The one thing the evidence does not settle is at the bottom.

## Vowels

The plain five. This is the most common vowel system on Earth — Spanish,
Japanese, Swahili, Indonesian, Greek, Hausa and Turkish all use it, and a
speaker of any of them already knows these five sounds.

| | Sound | As in | Amadunia |
|---|---|---|---|
| **a** | /a/ | Spanish *casa*, Japanese *aka*, Swahili *baba* | *ama* |
| **e** | /e/ | Spanish *peso*, Japanese *eki*, Indonesian *desa* | *beri* |
| **i** | /i/ | Spanish *sí*, Japanese *ichi*, Swahili *nini* | *mi* |
| **o** | /o/ | Spanish *como*, Japanese *oto*, Indonesian *toko* | *dom* |
| **u** | /u/ | Spanish *luna*, Japanese *uma*, Swahili *kuku* | *yu* |

They never change. There is no long and short, and **no reduced variant away
from stress**: the *a* in *ama* and the *a* in *salam* are the same sound, and so
are both unstressed *a*'s in a-ma-**DU**-nia. [Stress](stress.md) makes a
syllable louder, not different — the way it works in Indonesian, Swahili and
Japanese, and not the way it works in English, Russian or Portuguese, where an
unstressed vowel collapses toward a schwa. If it collapsed here, *written as
spoken* would stop being true the moment a word was said aloud.

That rule read "no reduced variant under stress" until September 5, 2026,
which names the wrong condition: a vowel that reduces reduces when it is
*un*stressed. As written it promised something no language on Earth breaks,
while every example under it — and the whole paragraph it belongs to — is about
the unstressed syllable. [stress.md](stress.md), [the phrasebook](../phrasebook.md)
and this page's own next sentence all had it the right way round. The page that
defines the twenty sounds is the one that had it backwards, in the six bold
words that state the rule.

## Two that need saying out loud

**`g` never softens.** In Italian, Spanish and French a *g* changes value
before *e* and *i*. In Amadunia it does not, because no letter changes value by
position — that is the rule. *genc* begins like *go*, not like *gem*.

**`s` never voices.** English turns *s* into /z/ between vowels — *rose*,
*music*. Amadunia does not. *pesa*, *risi* and *musik* all have the *s* of
*sun*.

Both follow from "one letter, one sound" and neither is obvious to a speaker
whose own language does the opposite, which is exactly why they are written
here.

## Aspiration, and other things that do not matter

Whether *p*, *t* and *k* are said with a puff of air is not distinguished.
*pan* said the English way and *pan* said the Spanish way are the same word.
The language does not use aspiration to tell any two words apart, and it never
will, because that would break the no-minimal-pairs rule.

The same goes for whether *t* and *d* are said against the teeth or the ridge
behind them. Say them the way your own language does.

## Open questions

- **How `r` is made.** A tap (Spanish *pero*, Japanese, Turkish, Indonesian), a
  trill (Spanish *perro*, Italian), or an approximant (English)? The language
  has never said, and the source words point in several directions at once —
  *rat* is Indo-Aryan, *rabota* Slavic, *risi* Mediterranean, *rafiki* Swahili.
  The tap is the safest of the three: it is what Spanish, Japanese, Turkish,
  Indonesian, Swahili and Hindi speakers already do, and it is easier for
  everyone else than a trill. But it is a decision and it has not been made.
  Until it is, any *r* is understood — nothing in the language distinguishes
  two words by their *r*.
