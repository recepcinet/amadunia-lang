# Negation — saying "not"

*First met in [Lesson 01](../lessons/lesson-01-greetings.md) as an answer; taught as a rule in [Lesson 14](../lessons/lesson-14-not-and.md).*

*Status: settled — decided September 3, 2026. Reversible if the founder disagrees; see "Why this and not the alternatives" below.*

## The rule

**Put *no* in front of what you are denying.** It is the same *no* you already answer questions with. Nothing else changes.

| | |
|---|---|
| Mi kula pan. | I eat bread. |
| Mi **no** kula pan. | I do not eat bread. |
| Mi **no** es doktor. | I am not a doctor. |
| Dom **no** kabir. | The house is not big. |
| Mi **no** sini. | I am not here. |

*no* goes immediately before the predicate — the verb, the adjective, the place word, or *es*.

## With tense

*no* comes first, then the tense particle, then the verb. Negation covers the whole thing:

| | |
|---|---|
| Mi **no suda** kula. | I did not eat. |
| Yu **no saufa** lai. | You will not come. |
| Dom **no suda** kabir. | The house was not big. |
| Ta **no saufa es** doktor. | She will not be a doctor. |

Never *suda no*. The order is always **no → tense → verb**.

## Answering is unchanged

*no* keeps its first job exactly as it was:

> — Yu mau ca?
> — No.

> — Yu mau ca?
> — No, mi no mau ca. Mi mau akua.

One word, two jobs, no new form for either.

## Sources

*no* comes from the deepest and most widely shared negation on Earth — the root behind Spanish and Italian *no*, English *no* and *not*, Portuguese *não*, Latin *non* and *ne*, Russian *ne*, Polish *nie*, Serbian *ne*, Greek, Sanskrit *na*, Hindi and Urdu *na* and *nahī̃*, Bengali *na*, Persian *na-*, Nepali *na*. Well over three billion people already carry some form of it.

Using the answer word as the negator is what Spanish, Italian and Portuguese do: *No.* / *No vengo.* Nothing is borrowed that the language did not already have.

## Why this and not the alternatives

**Why not *na*.** *na* is the same root and was the first candidate. It is a minimal pair with five settled words — *no*, *ta*, *ya*, *ba*, *ca*. The collision with *no* is the fatal one: a negator one sound away from the word for "no", meaning almost the same thing, would be misheard constantly and the mistake would never be visible. The collision with *ta* is nearly as bad, since negation stands right beside pronouns.

**Why reuse *no* instead of adding a word.** It costs nothing, it is already in the founder's first word list, and it is the same root every rejected candidate was reaching for. A language whose whole claim is "no exceptions" should not carry two words for one idea.

**Why *no* before the tense particle.** Negation is about the whole statement, not about the time. Putting it outermost means the rule can be stated once — *no* goes in front of what you deny — and never needs a second clause.

| Candidate | Source | Reason rejected |
|---|---|---|
| na | Sanskrit, Hindi, Persian | minimal pair with *no*, *ta*, *ya*, *ba*, *ca* |
| ne | Latin, Russian, Polish, French | minimal pair with *ke* and *no* |
| la | Arabic *lā* | minimal pair with *ta*, *ya*, *ba*, *ca* |
| bu | Chinese *bù* 不 | minimal pair with *du*, *ba*, *yu* |
| nai | Japanese *nai* ない | minimal pair with *bai*, *fai*, *lai*, *nau* |
| non | Latin, French, Italian | no collision, but a second word for a job *no* already does |
| tidak | Indonesian | one family, and three syllables for the commonest word in any language |

## Open questions

- **Contrastive ellipsis — "X, not Y" — has no form.** *no* goes before the
  predicate, and the predicate is a verb, an adjective, a place word or *es*.
  It never goes before a noun. Five sentences were writing *X, no Y* anyway,
  dropping the verb out of the second half the way English does:

  | Where | Was | Now |
  |---|---|---|
  | [Lesson 19](../lessons/lesson-19-home-and-nature.md) | *…korku dari animal kabir, no dari kucing* | a second clause, with its verb |
  | [Lesson 20](../lessons/lesson-20-colours-and-health.md) | *Mi mau kamisa asul, no kamisa merah* | *Mi mau kamisa asul. Mi no mau kamisa merah.* |
  | [Lesson 21](../lessons/lesson-21-things-and-ideas.md) | *Anahtar mi in kamra, no in bage* | two sentences |
  | [the phrasebook](../phrasebook.md) | *Mi mau ini, no itu* | *Mi mau ini. Mi no mau itu.* |
  | [demonstratives.md](demonstratives.md) | the same sentence | the same |

  Every one of them is longer now, and a phrasebook line that a traveller
  actually says is longer than it needs to be. That is the argument **for**
  deciding this, and it is the founder's to make: a rule that lets *no* stand
  in front of a repeated constituent would give all five back at no cost in
  words. Nothing here decides it.

  A sixth sentence was a different fault and is simply fixed:
  [Lesson 22](../lessons/lesson-22-doing-and-feeling.md) said *No korku* for
  "no fear", which needs the existential — *No es korku*, there is no fear.

  Found September 5, 2026. The last of them had been promoted **into a rule
  page the day before**, when *Mi mau ini, no itu* was cited on
  [demonstratives.md](demonstratives.md) as evidence that a demonstrative can
  be an object. It is evidence of that. Nobody had looked at its *no*.



- ~~Answering a negative question~~ — settled: *ya* and *no* answer the **fact**, never the wording. *Yu no mau ca? — No* means I do not want it; *Ya* means I do. This is the English, Spanish, Hindi, Arabic, Turkish and Indonesian system, by far the majority. When it matters, answer with the verb: *Mi mau* / *Mi no mau*.
- **Nothing, nobody, never.** Not yet designed. Whether they are built from *no* plus an existing word, or given their own roots, is open.
