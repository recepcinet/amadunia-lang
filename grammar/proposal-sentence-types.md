# Proposal: the imperative and the fragment

**This is not a rule.** Nothing here has been adopted. It is the briefing for
the two questions [the open questions](README.md) place second, after
[stress](proposal-stress.md). They are put together because they are the same
question asked twice: what counts as a sentence when it is not a statement.

Both are already in use. Neither was ever granted.

## What is actually in use

**The imperative — 17 sentences.** Seven across Lessons 13, 16 and 19 and
the phrasebook, counted when this briefing was written; ten more in
[text 8](../texts/text-8-kaifa-suru-ca.md), a recipe written deliberately
without one to find out what that costs. That text is the sharpest evidence
available: every step of it says *yu* where the rest of the world says
nothing. The original seven, from the lessons and
[the phrasebook](../phrasebook.md):

```
Anda tarik ini.      Walk this road.
Go yamin.            Go right.
Buka ain yu!         Open your eyes!
Sema tena, plis.     Say it again, please.
Lai kan ta.          Come and see her.
```

The form in use is a **bare verb with no subject**. Lessons have written it
that way since Lesson 10 and no rule anywhere permits it.

**The fragment — 44 utterances** in dialogue and the phrasebook: *Ya, ok.*
*No, bas.* *Corba, plis.* *Sasa.* *Besok.* *Madad!* *Ke?* *Harga?*

## A distinction the count had to be corrected for

Counting fragments naively gives fifty-seven, and most are not fragments at
all. A grammar lesson that prints *dom mi*, *tri anak* or *nama yu* in a table
is **citing a phrase**, not claiming it is a sentence — the same way an English
grammar prints "the big house". Only utterances in conversation are the real
phenomenon, and there are 44 of them.

That distinction matters for whatever rule is written: it should govern what
may be *said alone*, not what may be *printed alone*.

## The imperative: the slot is free

This is the same shape as two decisions already made.

Existential *es* works because **Amadunia never drops a subject** — so a
sentence beginning with *es* can only be existential. Verb chains work because
verb-next-to-verb meant nothing before. In both cases the language took an
empty slot for nothing.

A sentence beginning with a **verb** is the same empty slot. Amadunia has no
subject-dropping, so a bare initial verb cannot be anything else. Ratifying
what is already written would cost no word and create no ambiguity.

The alternatives all cost something:

| Option | Who does it | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Bare verb — what is in use | Chinese, Indonesian, English, Swahili | nothing |
| A particle after the verb | Indonesian *-lah*, Japanese | a word, and a new position rule |
| Reuse *plis* as the marker | — | makes every command polite, which is not the same thing |
| A distinct verb form | Spanish, Arabic, Hindi | breaks "no word ever changes shape" |

The last is disqualified outright: a separate imperative form would be the
first inflection in the language.

## The fragment: what a rule would have to allow

Every language permits fragments; none of the source languages requires a full
sentence to answer a question. The options are about **how much** to permit:

| Option | What it says |
|---|---|
| Any constituent may stand alone as an utterance | Describes what already happens. Costs nothing. |
| Only answers and greetings may | Would make *Corba, plis* and *Harga?* illegal, and those are what a phrasebook is |
| Nothing may — full sentences only | Would make the phrasebook impossible and no language does it |

The first is almost certainly right, but it should be **written down** rather
than left to work by accident, because a learner reading the grammar today
finds nothing that lets them say *Sasa.*

## What a decision needs to say

For the imperative: the rule in one sentence; that no subject appears; whether
a subject *may* appear for emphasis (*Yu go!*); how negation works with it
(*No go* — presumably, since *no* precedes the predicate); and whether *plis*
has any grammatical role or is only polite.

For fragments: which constituents may stand alone; whether a fragment answer
inherits the question's tense (*— Yu suda kula? — Ya.*); and whether the
existing conversations in the lessons need any rewriting, which on this
evidence they do not.
