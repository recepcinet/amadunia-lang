# Tense

*Taught in [Lesson 04](../lessons/lesson-04-tense.md).*

*Status: settled — decided September 2, 2026. Reversible if the founder disagrees; see "Why this and not the alternatives" below.*

## The rule

**The verb never changes.** Time is shown by one invariant particle placed directly before the verb. The present is unmarked.

| Time | Particle | Example | English |
|---|---|---|---|
| present | — | Mi kula pan. | I eat bread. |
| past | **suda** | Mi **suda** kula pan. | I ate bread. |
| future | **saufa** | Mi **saufa** kula pan. | I will eat bread. |

That is the whole system. There is nothing to conjugate, no irregular verbs, and no agreement with the subject: *mi suda kula*, *yu suda kula*, *ta suda kula*, *kita suda kula* — the verb *kula* is the same word every time.

## Where the particle goes

Always immediately before the verb, after the subject.

- Ta **suda** lai dom. — She came home.
- Rafiki **saufa** nomu ca. — The friend will drink tea.
- Anak **suda** kan sol. — The child saw the sun.

Never after the verb, never at the end of the sentence.

## When the particle may be left out

Once the time is clear, it does not need to be repeated. If a sentence has already been placed in the past, the sentences that follow may drop *suda* until the time changes.

> Mi **suda** go dom. Mi kula pan. Mi nomu ca.
> — I went home. I ate bread. I drank tea.

Including the particle in every sentence is never wrong. Leaving it out is allowed only when the time has already been set.

## Sources

| Particle | Source | Meaning there |
|---|---|---|
| suda | Indonesian/Malay *sudah* | already, done — used exactly this way to mark completed action |
| saufa | Arabic *sawfa* (سوف) | the standard future particle in Modern Standard Arabic |

Two different families, neither already dominant in the vocabulary. Both are semantically transparent in their source: *suda* literally says "already", *saufa* literally says "will".

## Why this and not the alternatives

**Why particles, not endings.** Design rule 3 forbids conjugation. A particle is a separate word that never changes; an ending would attach to the verb and, in every natural language that has them, eventually breeds irregulars.

**Why an unmarked present.** It is the most common tense in speech and the one a beginner needs first. Making the default the shortest form is what Chinese, Indonesian, Malay, Thai and Vietnamese all do, and it is the single feature that makes those languages easy to start.

**Why two particles, not one.** Some languages mark only "not-present" and let context sort past from future. That saves a word but costs clarity, and clarity is cheaper.

**Candidates rejected, and why:**

| Candidate | Source | Reason rejected |
|---|---|---|
| akan (future) | Indonesian *akan* | metathesis pair with *anak* (child) — a-k-a-n vs a-n-a-k |
| ta (future) | Swahili *-ta-* | *ta* is already he/she |
| ga (future) | Hindi *-gā* | minimal pair with *go*, the verb it would most often stand next to |
| hui (future) | Chinese *huì* | introduces the vowel sequence *ui*, not yet attested in the language |
| le (past) | Chinese *le* | one light syllable, easy to miss in speech; and Chinese *le* is post-verbal, so the borrowing would misrepresent the source |
| mau (future) | Indonesian colloquial | *mau* is already "to want"; using it for both breeds ambiguity |
| go (future) | English "going to" | same problem: *go* is already "to go" |

## Open questions

- Whether *suda* also covers the perfect ("I have eaten") or whether that needs its own marker.
- Whether a habitual ("I eat every day") ever needs marking, or whether the unmarked present carries it.
