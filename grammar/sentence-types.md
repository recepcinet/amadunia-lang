# Commands and fragments

*Fragments are taught in [Lesson 01](../lessons/lesson-01-greetings.md), where the first conversation is made of them; commands in [Lesson 10](../lessons/lesson-10-a-day.md), where the first one appears.*

*Status: settled — decided September 3, 2026.*

Two rules, decided together because they are the same question asked twice:
what counts as a sentence when it is not a statement.

## A command is a verb with no subject

**A sentence that begins with a verb and has no subject is a command.**

| | |
|---|---|
| Kula pan. | Eat bread. |
| Anda tarik ini. | Walk this road. |
| Go yamin. | Go right. |
| Buka ain yu! | Open your eyes! |
| Otur sini. | Sit here. |

**No word was added and no word changed shape.** The slot was empty and the
language took it, exactly as it did with the existential *es* and with verb
chains. Amadunia never drops a subject, so a sentence starting with a verb
could not have been anything else. Fourteen sentences in the lessons, the
phrasebook and text 4 were already written this way, since Lesson 10, and not
one of them needs rewriting.

### No subject, ever

*Yu go* is "you go" — a statement, however forcefully it is said. A command has
no subject and that is what makes it one. Emphasis is carried by the voice and
the exclamation mark, not by putting the pronoun back:

| | |
|---|---|
| Go! | Go! |
| Yu go. | You go. — a statement about you |

This is the line that keeps the rule free of exceptions. Letting a subject
appear "for emphasis" would make *Yu go* two sentences at once, and the
language has spent three hundred roots avoiding exactly that.

### The one verb it does not apply to

*es* is a verb — [copula.md](copula.md) says so, and it takes the tense
particles like any other. So *Es doktor* is a sentence beginning with a verb
and no subject, and by the rule above it should be a command: *be a doctor*.

It is not. **A sentence-initial *es* is the existential**, settled first and
standing in **44** sentences: *Es hotel in madina*, there is a hotel in the
city. That read thirty until September 5, 2026, counted the day the rule was
made and not since; [`check.py`](../check.py) counts it now, over the lessons,
the texts and the phrasebook. The narrower rule wins over the broader one, which is how any two rules
that overlap have to be ordered, and it is not an exception carved out for a
word — *es* is simply already spoken for in that position.

The cost is named in the open questions below: there is no way to tell someone
to **be** something.

### Negation needs no new rule

*no* goes in front of what it denies, which in a command is the verb.

| | |
|---|---|
| No kula pan. | Do not eat bread. |
| No go! | Do not go! |

### *plis* is politeness, not grammar

*Kula, plis* is a polite command; *Kula* is a command. *plis* adds nothing
grammatical, marks nothing, and may be left out of any command without changing
what the sentence is.

## Any constituent may stand alone

**A phrase may be a whole utterance when what it answers is clear.**

| | |
|---|---|
| — Yu mau ke? — Ca. | — What do you want? — Tea. |
| — Kab? — Besok. | — When? — Tomorrow. |
| Corba, plis. | Soup, please. |
| Madad! | Help! |
| Harga? | Price? |

Forty-four of these are already in the lessons and the phrasebook. The rule
describes them; it does not change one.

**Said alone, not printed alone.** A grammar table that prints *dom mi* or *tri
anak* is citing a phrase, the way an English grammar prints "the big house". It
is not claiming that a phrase is a sentence, and this rule has nothing to say
about it. Counting naively gives fifty-seven fragments; forty-four is the count
of things anybody actually said.

**Those two numbers cannot be reproduced, and the reason is the distinction
itself.** Separating a phrase somebody said from a phrase a table cites is a
judgement about the page it sits on, and no rule stated here makes it
mechanical — which is why the sentence needs two numbers at all. A scan of
utterances with no predicate, taking a quoted turn or a phrasebook row as
"said", gives 74 and 30 rather than 57 and 44. That is a different method, not
a correction, and it is recorded rather than substituted: replacing a figure
with one from a definition invented to check it is how a page ends up with a
number no method produces. The existential count above **is** mechanical — a
sentence whose first word is *es* — and is checked.

**A fragment answer inherits the question.** *— Yu suda kula? — Ya.* means yes,
I ate. The tense, the subject and the verb all come from the question, and
nothing needs to be repeated or marked.

## Where the two rules touch

A bare verb is a command. A fragment may be any constituent, and a verb is one.
So *Kula* alone is a command at the start of an exchange and an answer in the
middle of one:

> — Yu mau suru ke?
> — Kula.

That is context doing the work, and it is what English does with *Eat.* too.
It is the one place these two rules overlap, and it is written here rather than
left to be discovered.

## Sources

Bare-verb commands: Chinese, Indonesian, Malay, English, Swahili, Turkish
(*git*), Persian. It is the majority pattern on Earth and the one that costs
nothing.

Fragments: every language. None of the source languages requires a full
sentence to answer a question.

## Rejected

**A particle after the verb** — Indonesian *-lah*, Japanese. Costs a word and a
new position rule to say what word order already says.

**Reusing *plis* as the marker.** It would make every command polite, and a
warning is not a request.

**A distinct verb form** — Spanish *come*, Arabic, Hindi. Disqualified outright:
it would be the first inflection in a language whose second design rule is that
no word ever changes shape.

**Requiring a full sentence** — no fragments. No language does this, and it
would make the phrasebook impossible to write.

## Open questions

- **Whether a command can be softened or strengthened by grammar.** English has
  *do sit down* and *just go*; Amadunia has the voice, the exclamation mark and
  *plis*, and nothing else. Whether that is enough is untested.
- **There is no way to command someone to *be* something.** *Es doktor* is
  taken by the existential — there is a doctor — so *be a doctor*, *be quick*,
  *be careful* have no form. Every language has these and they are among the
  commonest things anyone says. Found on September 3, 2026 by asking what the
  command rule does to *es*, the day the command rule was made. **Evidenced by
  writing on September 4**: [text 12](../texts/text-12-tarik-por-skola.md), a
  mother walking a child to a road, wanted it three times on one page — *be
  careful*, *be quick*, *do not be afraid* — and wrote two commands, a time
  word, and nothing at all in their places. *Do not be afraid* fails twice
  over, because *korku* is a noun as well.
