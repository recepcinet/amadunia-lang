# Questions

*Taught in [Lesson 07](../lessons/lesson-07-questions.md).*

*Status: settled — decided September 2, 2026. Reversible if the founder disagrees; see "Why this and not the alternatives" below.*

## Yes / no questions: rising tone, written with ?

A yes/no question is a statement said with a rising tone and written with a question mark. Nothing is added, nothing moves.

| Statement | Question |
|---|---|
| Yu mau ca. | Yu mau ca? |
| Dom yu kabir. | Dom yu kabir? |
| Ta suda lai. | Ta suda lai? |

This is what the language has done since its first sentence (*Hi! Ok?*). It is confirmed, not changed.

## Question words: stay where the answer goes

To ask *what, who, where, when, why, how, how many*, put the question word **in the exact slot the answer would occupy**. Word order does not change.

| Question | Answer | |
|---|---|---|
| Yu kan **ke**? | Yu kan anak. | What do you see? — You see a child. |
| **Kim** lai? | Rafiki lai. | Who is coming? — A friend is coming. |
| Yu go **nali**? | Mi go dom. | Where are you going? — I'm going home. |
| Yu saufa lai **kab**? | Mi saufa lai din. | When will you come? — I'll come by day. |
| Yu mau ca **porke**? | — | Why do you want tea? |
| Yu go **kaifa**? | Mi go taksi. | How do you go? — I go by taxi. |
| Yu kan **berapa** anak? | Mi kan tri anak. | How many children do you see? — I see three. |

Read the question and the answer side by side: they are the same sentence with one word swapped. That is the whole rule.

## The seven words

| Word | Meaning | Source |
|---|---|---|
| ke | what | Spanish *qué*, Portuguese *que*, Italian *che*, French *que* |
| kim | who | Turkish, Azeri, Uzbek, Kazakh *kim* |
| nali | where | Chinese *nǎlǐ* 哪里 |
| kab | when | Hindi/Urdu *kab*, Bengali *kobe* |
| porke | why | Spanish *por qué*, Portuguese *por que*, Italian *perché*, French *pourquoi* — all literally "for what" |
| kaifa | how | Arabic *kayfa* كيف |
| berapa | how many, how much | Indonesian/Malay *berapa* |

Six families in seven words. Each was chosen to be recognised on sight by the largest possible number of people while no one family supplies more than two.

## Two details

**berapa behaves like a number.** It sits where a number sits — before the noun — and, like a number, leaves the noun single: *berapa anak*, never *berapa anak-anak*. This follows from the plural rule; nothing new.

**A question word needs no rising tone.** *Kim lai?* is already a question because *kim* is in it. The question mark is written; the voice may rise or not.

## Sources for the two rules

*Rising tone for yes/no* is the primary or a fully standard method in Spanish, Portuguese, Italian, Russian, Hindi, Indonesian, Persian, Hebrew, Greek and every Arabic dialect — most of the world outside East Asia.

*Question word in place* (no fronting) is how Chinese, Japanese, Korean, Turkish, Hindi, Indonesian, Tamil, Persian and Swahili ask: *ni kan shénme?* (you see what?), *sen ne gördün?* (you what saw?), *kamu lihat apa?* (you see what?). By speaker count it is the majority pattern on Earth. English, Romance and Arabic move the question word to the front instead; that movement is a rule with its own exceptions in every language that has it, and it is the one thing this design leaves out.

## Why this and not the alternatives

**Why no yes/no particle.** Every particle with real reach collides with a word already settled — and not just one or two of them:

| Candidate | Source | Collision |
|---|---|---|
| ma | Chinese *ma* 吗 | minimal pair with *ba*, *ca*, *mi*, *ta*, *ya* |
| ka | Japanese *ka* か, Korean *-kka* | minimal pair with *ba*, *ca*, *ke*, *ta*, *ya* — five at once |
| mi | Turkish *mi* | is already "I" |
| ne | Latin *-ne*, Japanese *ne* | minimal pair with *ke*, *no* |
| mai | Thai *mái* | minimal pair with *bai*, *fai*, *lai*, *mal*, *mau* |
| ba | Chinese *ba* 吧, Tagalog *ba* | is already 8 |
| ni | Korean *-ni*, Yoruba *ni* | minimal pair with *hi*, *mi*, *no* |
| kya | Hindi *kya* | doubles as "what"; two jobs for one word |
| hal | Arabic *hal* | minimal pair with *hao*, *mal* — good and bad at once |
| apa | Indonesian *apa(kah)* | minimal pair with *ama* |

Ten candidates from ten families, ten collisions. As with possession, the language is saying it does not want a word here — and it does not need one, because rising tone is already attested from the first lesson and is standard across most of the world. A tonal-language speaker who finds rising intonation awkward can rely on the question mark in writing and on context in speech, exactly as they do in colloquial Mandarin.

**Why in-place, not fronting.** Fronting changes word order, and Amadunia has exactly one word order. In-place keeps it. It is also the majority pattern by speakers and the easier one to learn: the learner builds the statement they already know and swaps one word.

**Candidates for the seven words that were rejected:**

| Candidate | Meaning | Reason rejected |
|---|---|---|
| ne (Turkish) | what | minimal pair with *ke*, *no* — including the *ke* that was chosen for the same job |
| che (Persian) | what | not spellable: *c* is already the *ch* sound and the alphabet has no digraph. Written legally as *ce* it is a minimal pair with *ca* and *ke* |
| mana (Indonesian) | where | minimal pair with *mama*, *mano* |
| do (Japanese) | how | minimal pair with *du*, *go*, *no* |
| kak (Russian) | how | minimal pair with *kab*, *kan* |
| kam (Arabic) | how many | minimal pair with *kab*, *kan*, *kim* |
| kitne (Hindi) | how many | **reason withdrawn — see below** |
| kuando / kuanto (Spanish) | when / how many | contain *uan* (1); and would be a minimal pair with each other |
| kapan (Indonesian) | when | ends in *pan* (bread) |
| itsu (Japanese) | when | sound, not sense: recognised by ~125M against *kab*'s ~700M |

*kab* and *berapa* each share a first syllable with a settled word (*kabir*, *beri*). Neither is a minimal pair — the words differ in length and stress — and both were kept for their reach.

### The collisions were recounted on September 6, 2026

Both tables above were recomputed against the dictionary, one candidate at a
time. **Two rows were exact** — *ka* against five, and *ce* against *ca* and
*ke*. **Eight named one collision where there were two, three or five**: *ne*
collides with *ke* as well as *no*, *mai* with *bai*, *fai*, *lai* and *mal* as
well as *mau*, *kam* with *kab* and *kim* as well as *kan*. Every row names all
of them now.

Two of the eight were understated in a way that mattered more than arithmetic.
*hal* was rejected for being "close to *hao*" and *apa* for sitting "inside
*papa*" — a resemblance and a substring, neither of which is a rule here. Both
are outright minimal pairs, *hal* with *hao* and *mal* both, and *apa* with
*ama*. The decisive reason was available and a softer one was written down.

And one row's reason does not hold at all. **"*kitne* begins with *kita*"** —
it does not: *kitne* is k-i-t-n-e and *kita* is k-i-t-a. *kitne* has no minimal
pair in the dictionary and contains no root, so the page now offers no reason
for rejecting it. That is left as a gap in the record rather than filled in:
*berapa* is settled and nothing here reopens it, but the reason a candidate was
turned away is the part of a decision worth keeping, and this one was never
true.

The most pointed of the corrections is the smallest. The Turkish *ne* was
rejected for colliding with *no*; it also collides with *ke*, the word this
page chose for the same job. Both are "what", one letter apart.

**It is not only this page.** The check written for these two tables was
pointed at every rejection table in `grammar/`, and it found the same habit in
six more: *ga* in [tense.md](tense.md) named *go* and collides with five; *ki*
in [subordination.md](subordination.md) named *ke* and collides with three;
[place.md](place.md) under-named nine rows of fifteen. Of the **51 such rows in `grammar/`, 23 across seven pages named one
collision where the dictionary held more**, and none of them named a collision
that was not real — the reasons were true and
short, never wrong. Every row is complete now, and the checker requires the
whole set rather than a member of it.

## Open questions

- **May a question word stand in a modifier's slot?** The swap rule produces it
  without anyone deciding: the answer is *Libro ini*, this book, the unknown is
  the *ini*, and swapping it gives *Libro ke?* — which
  [Lesson 16](../lessons/lesson-16-school-and-time.md) writes and glosses
  "Which book?". English does the same with *what book*, so nothing looks
  wrong, and the language has no word for *which*. But this page describes the
  question word as standing where an **answer** stands, and *ini* is a modifier
  rather than an answer. One sentence in the whole course does it. Found
  September 5, 2026 while checking that a question word used as a noun
  predicate takes *es*; that check exempts the two-word case for exactly this
  reason, and if the answer is no, *Libro ke?* has to be rewritten.



- ~~**The imperative was never granted.**~~ — granted September 3, 2026 in [sentence-types.md](sentence-types.md): a sentence that begins with a verb and has no subject is a command, and that is also what tells it from a statement. *Anda tarik ini*, *Buka ain yu*, *Otur sini* stand unchanged. Found by [the fourth text](../texts/text-4-in-madina-baru.md).
- ~~**Fragments.**~~ — settled the same day in [sentence-types.md](sentence-types.md): any constituent may stand alone as an utterance. *Ke?* *Harga?* *Ponte?* are legal by rule now rather than by accident, and a fragment answer inherits the tense and subject of the question.

- ~~Adverb position~~ — settled in [place.md](place.md): place first, then time, both at the end. *kab, porke, kaifa* stay where they are.
- **Lesson 6 asked *Nama yu?*** with no question word. That is a yes/no-shaped question that context turns into "what is your name". The explicit form is *Nama yu es ke?* Both are allowed; whether the short form should be discouraged in teaching is open.
